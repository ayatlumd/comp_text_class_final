"If I get a chance to be promoted, why not," said Qhimed Yudron, the 37-year-old director of political and legal committee in Chengguan district, Lhasa, Southwest China's Tibet Autonomous Region. The first female director in her district, Lhasa's most populous, Qhimed told the Global Times that her superiors think she is "more careful, considerate, and good at listening" because she is a woman. The number of female ethnic-minority officials in national and local government bodies is gradually increasing, under a drive for greater gender equality and diversity in decision making. After Bu Xiaolin, a 58-year-old ethnic Mongol, was appointed as a vice chairman and acting chairman of North China's Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region in late March, two out of the five ethnic-minority autonomous regions in China are now headed by minority women, a situation that is unprecedented since the country was established in 1949. Since the 1990s, about five minority women have been appointed to official posts at provincial-level government bodies or above, and currently over 30 minority women are working as vice-provincial officials, people.cn reported on Wednesday. 
Among them are Uyunqimg, a vice chairwoman of the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress (NPC) from 2003 to 2013, the first and so far only minority woman to work as a "national leader," and Fu Ying, chairwoman of the Foreign Affairs Committee of the NPC, said the report. Personal choice Qhimed told the Global Times that more and more minority women have been given official positions in Lhasa, and there is one simple reason she has worked for the government since graduating in 2001, "I am happy at my work, though there is a lot of pressure, but I am doing something good of society." Doing something for the good of the country is a mission shared by many other ethnic-minority officials, who said that the country's future must be decided through the participation of more women from different backgrounds. "China has done a great job in gender equality including policy support and eliminating discrimination, better than many other countries," said Buwajar Abla, a deputy at the the regional and national committee of the CPPCC, China's top political advisory body for 14 years. She told the Global Times that one reason she was promoted is because she is a Uyghur woman.Abla noted that the voices of minority women are increasingly being heard.For example, Abla handed in a proposal to the CPPCC national committee which called for a greater anti-extremist measures in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region, an area in which many women suffer due to extremist sentiment, being forced to wear burqas and denied education."However, very few female officials are working in senior positions, such as provincial-level or national-level posts, partially for institutional reasons and sometimes out of their own choice, said Abla. Officials in Tibet face extra pressure as they deal with not only developmental issues but also regional stability, which is especially difficult as many women want to balance their work with life, said Qhimed. National drive Last October, a national meeting that stressed the selection, training and promotion of young and female officials, as well as officials from ethnic minority groups and non-Party members, was chaired by Liu Yunshan, a member of the Standing Committee of the Political Bureau of the Communist Party of China (CPC) Central Committee. In the following months, similar meetings were held in regions including Xinjiang, Shandong and Gansu provinces. Sun Xiaoli, an expert on female leadership with the Chinese Academy of Governance, noted that women officials are especially needed in regions populated with minority groups, where officials who can work with and listen to locals are essential. According to a 2013 Xinhua News Agency report, the ratio of female NPC deputies reached 23.4 percent, or 699 out of a total of 2,987 lawmakers that year, the highest ever, and minority women made up 41.3 percent of ethnic minority deputies. Among the 396 current members of provincial CPC standing committees, 34 are women, seven are from an ethnic minority, said Ta Kung Pao.
