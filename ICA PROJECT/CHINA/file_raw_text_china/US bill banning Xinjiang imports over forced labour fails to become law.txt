When the spending bill and ­coronavirus relief package was ­unveiled in the United States ­Congress on Monday just hours before its passage, it set off the usual scramble among policy ­observers to find out which ­unrelated bills had been bolted on to the must-pass legislation.
Among the amendments, known as "riders", were measures strengthening US support for ­human rights in Tibet, creating public museums to honour the history of women and Latinos, and protecting insured patients from surprise medical bills.
But for advocates of a stronger US challenge of Beijing's human rights record, there was one prominent omission: legislation that would ban all imports from China's Xinjiang region over concerns about forced labour there.
The bill, the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act, was ­awaiting approval in the Senate, having breezed through the House of Representatives in ­September by a 406-3 vote.
But with the last major legislative order of this congressional session concluded, any hope the bipartisan support for a stronger US policy to counter Beijing would see the forced labour bill enacted this year has been ­extinguished.
After the approval of the omnibus spending bill and coronavirus relief package, lawmakers have begun returning home for the winter break.
They are expected to return to Capitol Hill only if President Donald Trump rejects the military budget bill passed this month, ­requiring a congressional vote to override the veto.
It is possible lawmakers will see to other legislative business should they return for an override vote, but it is unlikely they would touch hefty foreign policy issues.
"Unfortunately there is no more movement in the Senate," said a senior congressional staff member close to the forced ­labour legislation, speaking on condition of anonymity. "We will try again right out of the gate in the new Congress."
Introduced in March, the forced labour bill would prohibit US importers from sourcing any goods from Xinjiang unless they could prove forced labour was not involved in their production.
That would reverse the current calculus of US customs, which seizes imports if there is evidence they were produced using forced labour.
Beijing denies the existence of forced labour in Xinjiang.
The legislation provoked lobbying efforts from corporations and trade groups worried about its impact on their supply chains.
The bill's backers had committed to rejecting any pressure that would have hollowed out the act's core provisions, preferring to ­reintroduce the bill in the next ­session rather than squeeze through a watered down bill.
"Any weakening of this bill in the Senate, we will not accept it in the House," Representative Jim McGovern, a Massachusetts Democrat said. "And then we will be forced to come back early next year and do this again."
