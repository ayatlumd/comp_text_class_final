One of the reasons that an increasing number of young people love avant-garde brands, such as Supreme, ACEG and Stay Real to name a few, is the concept they represent: Anyone can be fashionable. It was such an idea that inspired two young Uyghur men from Northwest China's Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region to establish their own avant-garde brand that interweaves traditional and modern culture and Eastern and Western fashion: Rameyda. While most people tend to think of Xinjiang as a very conservative and traditional region of China, Muradil Muhtar and Akbar Weixiaojiang are looking to change this image. Your own judge One of the Rameyda brand's more popular products is a black T-shirt featuring a tribal tattoo design inspired by the various coat of arms in the hit HBO drama Game of the Thrones. 
The design of the T-shirt is modeled on the neckline and embroidery patterns found on the traditional Uyghur shirt known as the kanway, while the tribal tattoo comes in two forms, a goat's head for men and a rose for women. On one of the sleeves, the word Dopeway - a combination of the words kanway and dope - is printed in English. Another popular item is a sweater shirt with the image of a Uyghur man wearing an astronaut helmet playing the traditional Uyghur instrument the tamboura. "It implies that we are yearning to explore fresh ideas," Weixiaojiang told the Global Times.Muhtar's favorite is a cap with the number 65 on it. According to the young man in his early 20s, the ID number of every Chinese citizen who is born in Xinjiang begins with 65, therefore the number represents the people of Xinjiang. Weixiaojiang, a 23-year-old high school dropout who has worked both in and outside Xinjiang as a manager of a milk tea shop, a car salesman and a real estate agent, told the Global Times that the idea to establish an avant-garde brand came suddenly. "We are both hip hop lovers and fans of avant-garde brands," Weixiaojiang said. Deciding to combine Muhtar's experience as a graphic designer with Weixiaojiang management experience, the two childhood friends established Rameyda in May, 2015. "Rameyda is a completely made-up word that represents our concept of 'Only You Can Judge You,'" Muhtar explained. Experimental fashion During the first half of 2015, Rameyda sold a total of 160 items. The next year, sales jumped to 480, and by December of this year sales rose to 1,860. Though not a big number, the two are happy that these sales have been able to keep their business running. Currently they run their business out of a shop in Urumqi, and handle online sales through Taobao and WeChat. "It is difficult for people who run avant-garde brands to persist for long. Many of them may only bring out one or two designs in a year and quit shortly after," Weixiaojiang told the Global Times. The major obstacle for these brands is still consumers' overwhelming preference for big name brands over newer contenders. According to Weixiaojiang, this situation is even more severe in Xinjiang than it is in the rest of China. Yet, he also pointed out that the desire for something new and innovative is becoming a major trend among young people in Xinjiang. Weixiaojiang recalled one time when they were asked to design a uniform for a class of middle school students. After learning about the two's backgrounds, each of the students sent them text messages expressing their support "which we found really comforting," he said. Last year, three new members joined the Rameyda team. All college students, two of which are ethnic Kazak. The increasing popularity of hip hop in Xinjiang may be one of the factors behind Rameyda's increasing sales, as a large portion of this music genre's fans are also fan of avant-garde culture. "The influence of hip hop in Xinjiang is far greater than in the rest of China," Kurbanjan Samat, a Xinjiang culture promoter and author of the book I Come from Xinjiang, told the Global Times. "Xinjiang was a major crossroads on the ancient Silk Road and is now a vital area serving the grand Road and Belt Initiative. It has always been tolerant of other cultures, which is why Xinjiang people are more willing to accept new things than people from other places." Samat explained that back in the 1980s, people in Urumqi would dress up in western suits and go watch Shakespeare plays. "You will find that young people here are far more fashionable than their peers in Shanghai and Beijing if you come to Urumqi today," he said.
