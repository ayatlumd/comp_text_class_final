Not far from the scalding sands of the Tarim Basin in Northwest China is the region's political and commercial center, Kashi. What Marco Polo called Cascar and the Chinese now refer to as Kashi, the distant outpost has fashioned itself over the millennia into one of the Silk Road's most vital international crossroads linking China with northern Pakistan and eastern Afghanistan.Indeed, Kashi more closely resembles the Middle East than the Middle Kingdom. The city is a veritable tapestry of central Asian cultures, as reflected in its weekly bazaar.
(From top) Selling silk textiles at a Hotan souq; praying inside Kashi's Id Kah mosque
Approaching the massive market in Konasheher old town, one must dodge the merchant calls of kilinglar! (Turkish for "come!") while browsing the endless displays of hand-tempered household wares, rusty treasures (genie lamp, anyone?), outdated tape decks, and throngs of women shrouded in hijab headscarves burrowing through piles of shimmering silk.Xinjiang's predominant ethnicity, the Uyghur, flavor the region with their unique Turkish-influenced culture and devout Islamic faith. With more then 12 million Muslims in China, Xinjiang accounts for over half the national total.Kashi literally comes to a halt five times a day when the faithful respond to the calling of the Adhan and rush to Id Kah - the largest mosque in China - for a congregational series of Mecca-facing prostrations and prayer.Regardless of the traditional lifestyle of the Uyghur, Kashi itself has developed over the years into a white-tiled mercantile metropolis, where even its weekly bazaar is now held in a modernized indoor facility of thousands of identical stalls.This refinement leaves many travelers desiring something a bit more authentic.The answer to anyone dissatisfied by the tamer and more contemporary Kashi is Xinjiang's lesser-known, yet arguably more impressive, souq in Hotan, a day's scenic drive south along the lethally hot Taklimakan, the world's second-largest desert.The shaded, tree-lined oasis is renowned throughout China for its jade, silk and carpets: the three treasures of Hetian (as the Chinese spell it), which translates into "place that abounds in jade."The first sights and sites anyone will happen upon in Hotan are entire streets of jade dealers, either from their storefronts, on blankets spread on the ground, in the trunks of cars or right out of their pant pockets.The rabid riots of precious stone peddlers and prospective buyers haggling in their Turkish tongue over every size and color of jade imaginable add to the chaos that is only the beginning of this mad market.
(From top) Nan bread street cart in Kashi; boy riding a mule-drawn carriage through Hotan Photos: Tom Carter/GT
Extending countless kilometers in all four directions and literally screaming with commerce, the traffic-stopping souq takes over the city streets from dusk till dawn; ass-drawn carriages contend with grumbling buses and caravans of camels plod alongside modern sedans.Asphalt inevitably dissolves into dust, where livestock both alive and freshly slaughtered trample the dirt or turn it into crimson mud.Baying horses, mules and bulls excrete freely onto the ground while being industriously inspected by interested human parties.To a pulsating background score of blacksmiths pounding on their anvils and 200-beat-per-minute Arabic tabla drums, the souq segues into heaps of faux jewelry, henna hair dye, and cheap cosmetics ravaged by young, olive-skinned women who prefer arm-revealing Western fashion to their more conservatively concealed counterparts.Meanwhile, local men get a shave and their head scalped by an outdoor barber or browse for a new knife and an embroidered dopi cap.Throughout it all are commingled scents of exhaust, dust and smoke. There may not be as much bread in the whole of China as there is in Kashi, where one is oft tempted by pyramids of lightly seasoned nan or stacks of sesame-seed bagels, all fresh out of the oven.Scarlet slices of watermelon, Xinjiang's most abundant fruit, and pink peaches blushing like a child's cheeks are the perfect desert dessert.Lamb chaunr (kebab) slow-roasted throughout the day over sizzling coals, an undulating landscape of lamian noodles topped with peppers, tomatoes and garlic, goat's head soup, yellow mountains of pilaf rice, and boiling vats of satiating cinnamon tea, sipped on by exhausted vendors relaxing with a few chapters of the Koran, offer a welcome respite before returning to Hotan's bizarre bazaar.
