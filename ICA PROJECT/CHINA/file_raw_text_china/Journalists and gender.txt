Some media professionals are dedicated to raising gender awareness. Photo: IC
For the past four years Lu Xiaofei, the former editor-in-chief of the China Women's News newspaper and winner of the Changjiang Taofen Awards, the country's most respected journalism prize, has invited the editorial executives of Beijing newspapers to take part in a vote for "the news event of the year promoting gender equality." Organized by the China Women's News and the Media Research School with the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, the event asks participants to select news events based on their relevance to gender equality and their affect on public awareness of gender. "The purpose of voting is, of course not voting itself, but promoting gender awareness," Lu said. Here gender is not about sex, or biological - it is a person's self-representation, and how institutions respond to a person based on his or her gender presentation.Fostering awarenessIn a mediascape that still explicitly goggles at women with doctorates in science and engineering and makes fun of men who welcome the idea of domestic duties, this is certainly necessary.Lu said it was extremely important for newspaper executives to have an awareness of gender. "Many of them had little awareness of it before, which might create errors when they are in charge of overall journalistic practice," she said.She said the newspaper executives debated and argued about the shortlisted news events every time they gathered to vote, but she believes they are important discussions and that this is healthy. 
Ma Leijun, an official with UN Women who has been working on a gender mainstreaming initiative in China, told the Global Times that he has lectured executives of some Chinese television stations about gender.Yang Lan, perhaps the country's best-known television journalist and host, was also one of Ma's "students."In an early toothpaste commercial, she played herself, a busy career woman who was suffering from gum problems. After she was cured by using the right toothpaste, she hugged a child while the voice-over intoned: "Ready for the most important job."Ma got Yang to realize that, by suggesting a woman's biggest duty was raising a child, the commercial was stressing her biology over her competence at and interest in work. Also, like most commercials, it was a male doctor that offered her help."She said she hadn't been aware of that being a problem before, but the lesson quickly made sense to her," Ma said.Thus, in a new commercial that was released last year, we see Yang in a newsroom offering dental advice to a younger male colleague. And there are no references to her identity as a mother or wife.
A journalist at work Photo: IC
Many volunteersWhilst some of the country's media professionals might start to develop gender awareness quite late in their careers, there are many younger journalists who have volunteered to take part in workshops on media and gender equality over the past few years. Like the "Gender and China" media reporting workshop in Guangzhou in December 2014. The organizer was Women Awakening, a charity organization promoting gender equality and women's rights, and keynote speakers included Cai Yiping, a media and gender expert. Earlier this month, there was another workshop on gender and media that was attended by journalists from newspapers in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou as well as geographically diverse areas including Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region, the Inner Mongolia Auttonomous Region and Hainan Province.They were addressed in the workshop by a panel of experienced professionals in gender-related coverage, reviewed news examples, and discussed solutions. A key element in this training is distinguishing stereotypical portrayals and, importantly, avoiding them.Feng Yuan, a one-time journalist with the People's Daily, has been examining media representations of women for 20 years, and said the overall trend has been upward but there is still a long way to go."Latin America and North America have media that clearly challenges stereotypes, while only 5 percent of Asian media reports do this," Feng said referring to recent research.She stressed that the rise of the Internet and new media does not make the situation look any better. In fact, she said, in many cases Internet news has even stronger stereotyping."It is important that journalists can communicate and share their ideas and experiences, and work together to push society's awareness of gender forward and uproot the commonly-held stereotypes," Feng said.The workshop offered insight and guidance on a series of gender related topics, including writing about domestic violence, sexual violence, women politicians, and LGBT (lesbian, gay, bisexual and transgender) groups.The sexual violence session, for example, gave detailed instructions on what to avoid when writing about this - blaming the victim, identifying offenders as "migrant populations," and promoting sex conservatism, just to name a few.Inspirational modelsThe workshop also outlined some story ideas on domestic violence. There was a suggestion that, instead of just writing about women currently caught in awful, seemingly hopeless situations, journalists could write about women who have won divorces from abusive husbands and started new lives - offering inspirational models for those women frightened of breaking up their relationships.Ma agreed with the Global Times that a big problem with gender-related training for media professionals was that those who participated at workshops like this were usually already very conscious of gender equality, and the journalists who needed to understand the problem never showed up.Addressing this particular problem, Ma has begun working with the Communication University of China on a project that will integrate gender education into journalism schools around the country.Wu Weihua, an assistant professor at the Television and Journalism School with the Communication University of China is in charge of the project, and told the Global Times that about six years ago the university conducted a national survey which showed gender education was "really lacking" in journalism schools.He said that while leading journalism schools, like those in the Tsinghua and Fudan universities, offered compulsory or selective courses on gender theory and feminist theory, non-key national universities rarely did this."Many journalism professors said that when they were studying journalism overseas, there was at least a chapter about gender. But in China this part of education is missing," Wu said.The project has expanded over the years to cover 20 to 30 journalism schools, in Beijing and Shanghai as well as some provinces including Sichuan and Fujian. After some time trying to find a local university partner, Wu said, an Inner Mongolian Autonomous Region university will hopefully be on board this year.The idea is to educate journalism students through their professors. Each year, these professors attend one or two workshops at their schools, where speakers like Ma Leijun and gender experts gave presentations and suggested how they might "skillfully integrate gender-related content" into their lectures.Basic conceptsMa would talk about the basic concepts of gender, while the experts gave specific examples that the professors might want to use.Wu said the project does not ask journalism professors to immediately open a course on media and gender. "Instead, they add a few sessions on this topic, for two to four hours, in their classes on writing, photography, or film history," Wu said.Wu believed this was effective. "It is more likely to have an influence on the mindset and conduct of young students, rather than those who receive it later as part of their career training," he said."The ultimate goal is that when media majors graduate, they already have gender awareness and will apply it when they become media professionals," Wu said.
