Although it meant traveling across 23 Chinese provinces with an amateur film crew in a total of four months to document more than 100 traditional Chinese handicrafts and the stories of 199 craftsmen, 40-something entrepreneur-turned-director Zhang Jing said he didn't regret a single moment, as it was always his dream to produce a real and human record of his childhood idols."I was born in a rural village. The way the rural craftsmen in my village were adept at turning ordinary things into something magical always awed me as a child," he told the Global Times on Monday. The resulting five-episode documentary Xunzhao Shouyi (lit. In Search of Handicrafts), Zhang's first such work, has gone viral on Chinese social media platforms recently, garnering nearly 800,000 views so far on Chinese streaming and video sharing platform bilibili.com since its debut in late April. 
"Moving" is the most frequently used word in many of the comments about the film.Currently sitting at 8.7/10 on Chinese review platform Douban, In Search of Handicrafts has been praised by many Chinese viewers as "an unusually honest and human work."'They got a spirit'A tranquil atmosphere permeates every frame of the film as it shows craftsmen of various ages with heads held low as they skillfully work on their creations a step at a time. Ranging from Yingjisha knives in Northwest China's Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region to handmade paper in East China's Anhui Province, the series features dozens of the 144 "down-to-earth" traditional Chinese handicrafts the team filmed."I made it clear from the start that the handicrafts we were going to film should be down-to-earth products, which means items meant for daily use rather than those used for decoration only, such as egg carving," explained Zhang.While the film is an amateur production featuring self-written narration dubbed by Zhang himself, unsophisticated cinematography and even the occasional inclusion of the film crew into frame, this rustic style seems to go well with what Zhang wanted to express.In his opinion, most Chinese documentaries about craftsmanship portray craftsmen in a bland and artificial way. "Like they belong to an unprivileged group in the society… You don't see the light of pride in their eyes that I saw in the eyes of my interviewees," Zhang noted. Sometimes it took the team several days to capture the stories that excited them at just one location. "Those craftsmen have a certain spirit, but they never think about it and don't know how to express in words what this spirit really is," Zhang said. "A real craftsman never thinks of himself or herself as someone important or talented." In addition to the down-to-earth atmosphere that has captured audiences' attention, the film also serves as a valuable record of some of China's endangered handicrafts, such as Tantou Spring Festival paintings in Central China's Hunan Province. The last inheritor of this art form passed away just months after shooting on the film completed in late 2014."Who knows how many of these interviewees will be still alive or still carrying on with their craft if you were to go looking for them one day," Zhang said.Unexpected popularityThe popularity of the series has turned Zhang into something of an Internet celebrity. Three years ago, Zhang's business career hit a huge rough patch when his video company ran into financial troubles. Worried and stressed, Zhang recalled the serene feeling he had watching traditional craftsmen during his childhood, so he decided to follow his dream of recording stories about this group of people he had long admired. To fund the shoot, Zhang sold one of his properties in Beijing in 2014 - a move that surprised many people considering how the city's already high property prices kept skyrocketing at the time."I have had no source of income since 2013. There was no other way to finance the film…I originally hoped that we could turn a profit from this film," Zhang told the Global Times. Zhang managed to assemble a three-man team - a professional photographer surnamed Jiang, an assistant for Jiang named Yu Pan and He Sigeng, their driver. The three set off in May, 2014, but Jiang quit due to personal reasons a few days after their departure. Fortunately their driver, He, agreed to take over after Zhang taught him some elementary knowledge about how to use the camera."Luckily, He was interested in photography. Yu also had to take over some of Jiang's jobs," said Zhang, who was also responsible for the film's audio and all the final editing work, which took him 26 months to accomplish. When the film wrapped up in 2016, Zhang was unable to bring the series to the small screen as he originally planned. He approached more than 20 television stations, but was turned down by each of them simply because the film was "an amateur production." Zhang had no choice but to upload his film on Chinese video sharing platforms for free in April, a move that ended up bringing the film and his team unexpected public attention."I set off feeling I like a savior of traditional handicrafts and craftsmen, but I was actually saved by them," Zhang said, adding that the strength that he saw in his interviewees humbled him and kept him going.Despite all the difficulties he has run into, Zhang is currently planning on a sequel to the series. He explained, however, that the unexpected popularity of the film has actually changed his original plans."Investors have approached me, but many have requirements when it comes to time and content, which I don't like very much… Moreover, audiences will have higher expectations which means we have to keep the sequel's quality in mind," Zhang said.
