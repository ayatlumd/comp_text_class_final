I n May 2024, the parents of Ekpar Asat made a long journey to a prison in the Chinese region of Xinjiang.
Asat, who belongs to the Uyghur ethnic group, was detained by authorities in April 2016 on charges of "inciting ethnic hatred". The founder of a popular Uyghur-language website, he had recently returned from attending a leadership programme in the US.
The May visit was the first time Asat's parents had seen him in person since his disappearance, and he had lost so much weight he was "unrecognisable", his sister Rayhan later said.
The meeting lasted barely ten minutes, and was done through a glass window, according to human rights groups. The family was forced to speak in Chinese - a language Ekpar's parents barely speak - and not permitted to show emotion. "Prisoners have to be always happy," Rayhan tells the FT.
It has now been a decade since Asat was jailed, one of an estimated million people swept up in a crackdown on the Uyghurs and other Muslim minority groups in Xinjiang. Reports by human rights groups of "re-education" camps and forced labour led to an international outcry and sanctions on China.
In late 2019 the Chinese government announced they were closing the camps which had drawn so much condemnation. Shohrat Zakir, Xinjiang's governor, said detainees had "graduated".
But FT analysis suggests that the Chinese state's campaign of oppression against Uyghurs and their culture and identity has in fact entered a new phase. While many camps have shut, a vast network of prisons and detention centres remains, alongside pervasive surveillance and systems of coercive social control.
It shows Xinjiang has the world's highest prison detention capacity relative to its population size - evidence that authorities continue to rely on mass incarceration. Researchers and rights groups say repression in the region now extends towards the long-term remaking of Uyghur society.
Beijing has expanded labour transfer programmes that move Uyghurs into factory work elsewhere in the country - schemes UN experts say can amount to forced labour. This places multinational corporations that work in China in a challenging situation, as Beijing is also making it increasingly difficult and dangerous for companies to perform due diligence in their supply chains so they do not target Uyghur rights.
The camp system acted as a "catalyst" for policies including "birth prevention, parent-child separation, boarding schooling ... and labour transfer", says Adrian Zenz, director of China Studies at the Victims of Communism Memorial Foundation, a US non-profit.
Researchers say the campaign reflects Beijing's drive to assimilate Uyghurs into mainstream Han Chinese identity amid rising nationalism under President Xi Jinping.
Uyghurs' distinct language, culture and Islamic faith create a "degree of insecurity that has only intensified as China has begun taking this profoundly nationalistic turn", says historian Hannah Theaker. "They just want to force them to be Chinese," adds Peter Irwin, co-executive director of the Network for Uyghur Rights.
Xinjiang is also strategically important to Beijing, as it straddles trade routes linking China to Central Asia, the Middle East and Europe, while holding large reserves of coal, oil and gas. "They see it as an economic engine for the country," Irwin says.
China continues to frame its policies in Xinjiang as essential to security and stability. In a speech last year, Xi stressed the need for readiness in "combating terrorism". State media said Xi also called for ethnic groups to "develop a correct view" of the country, its history, nation, culture and religion.
China tightly controls information from the region, censoring online material, restricting travel and intimidating Uyghurs abroad through threats against relatives back home. But the FT was able to gather a picture of the current situation from human rights observers, members of the diaspora, and people who recently left China and witnessed the mechanisms used to control Uyghurs. Their accounts, alongside FT analysis, suggest authorities continue to rely on widespread detention, shortterm arrests and intimidation, even as the campaign enters a new stage centred on forced cultural assimilation, particularly in the heavily Uyghur areas of southern Xinjiang.
Five years after the US declared China's actions in the region a "genocide", Uyghur identity and culture remain under "existential threat", says Yalkun Uluyol of Human Rights Watch.
In a statement, China's foreign ministry said the allegations in this article are "completely inconsistent with the facts". It said its policies have raised millions out of poverty and increased life expectancy, winning "the heartfelt support and endorsement" of people in Xinjiang, "including the Uyghur people".
W hen hundreds of thousands of Uyghurs were swept up in mass arrests across Xinjiang from 2017 onwards, global attention focused on the "vocational training centres" that became the symbol of Beijing's crackdown.
But many others - those the state considered most "dangerous", experts say - were quietly channelled into the formal prison system.
These facilities spread across the region in parallel with the camps, largely beyond the world's notice. In one county in southern Xinjiang, leaked data shows nearly one in 25 people was imprisoned on terrorism-related charges.
Human Rights Watch says many people were not charged with a legally recognisable offence, with some sentenced without formal trials. And while most of the "re-education" camps have since closed, much of the wider detention system remains in place.
Using satellite imagery, government documents, local media reports and witness accounts, the FT assessed the gross floor area of 579 detention compounds across Xinjiang and tracked their expansion, closure or continued use from 2011 to the present. The analysis found that the region currently has capacity to detain around 627,000 people without overcrowding - almost one in 40 of the local population - making it the world's largest detention system relative to population.
To assess whether facilities remained operational, the FT examined signs of continued activity including vehicle movements and light emission. Witness testimony and local reporting helped identify whether compounds had closed or remained active.
Human rights researchers believe many facilities remain occupied. More than 578,000 people were prosecuted in Xinjiang between 2017 and 2022, according to figures compiled by the Uyghur Human Rights Project from official data. Researchers believe many of those sentenced are likely still imprisoned.
The figures suggest that if Xinjiang were a country, it would have the highest incarceration rate in the world, at 1,944 per 100,000 people. China's nationwide rate is 119 per 100,000 people, according to the World Prison Brief.
"The camps became the focus internationally," says Irwin of the Network for Uyghur Rights. "But prisons were always central to the system."
Accounts from former officials and residents now living abroad suggest people in the region continue to be detained, though at a lower level than during the peak years of 2017-18. A former police officer, Zhang Yabo, who had served in Xinjiang from 2014 to 2023, told the FT that security officials in the region had ordered a system of preemptive short-term detentions driven by quotas, with people taken to local facilities for days or weeks at a time.
Zenz, of the Victims of Communism Memorial Foundation, who has analysed Zhang's testimony, says the account suggests that with this policy of pre-emptive short-term detentions, Beijing has introduced a new, "recalibrated system of repression".
Uyghurs interviewed by the FT say large parts of their families have been detained. Nureli Abliz, a former telecoms worker in Xinjiang, estimates that roughly 70 per cent of his extended family remain in custody.
Zhang said that reasons given by the police to justify detention included household possession of so-called sensitive items including maps or personal fitness equipment. Activists in the diaspora continue to report former neighbours disappearing in the night, after being discovered with religious artefacts at home, for example, or fasting during Ramadan. Others have been detained after travelling abroad.
Human rights observers say that people have been detained for similar arbitrary reasons since the campaign began over a decade ago.
Among them was Asat, who was sentenced to 15 years and remains incarcerated in the city of Aksu. He was only discovered to be in prison four years after his disappearance when his sister Ray- han asked US lawmakers to write to the
Chinese Embassy in Washington DC. Of her parents, Rayhan says: "every time they return from Aksu, it takes weeks for them to recover."
China's foreign affairs ministry denied that any illegal detention or short-term detention system exists in Xinjiang, and that "vocational skills education and training centres" were introduced for a period of time to tackle terrorism and religious extremism. "China is a country governed by the rule of law," it said. It did not respond to questions about the network of prisons and detention centres, nor its extent.
The campaign to assimilate Uyghurs into mainstream Han Chinese culture stretches from cradle to grave.
Children are separated from their families in Xinjiang's vast boarding school system, justified by authorities as a way to provide education in rural areas. Uyghur activists say the removal of children from their families and communities is creating a "generation gap" in which many no longer understand their language and culture.
At university, Uyghur students are subjected to intensive monitoring, according to a former university worker. Counsellors monitor students constantly - justified in official university documents as a way to prevent "extremism" - and ensure they hold "correct" political views.
For other adults, the practice of forced labour, which provoked the US to impose sanctions on dozens of Chinese companies and ban imports, persists under a new guise.
Uyghurs have been sent hundreds or even thousands of miles away in recent years under state-organised labour schemes that Beijing says are intended to alleviate what it describes as "deep poverty" in southern Xinjiang.
State programmes pair eastern Chinese provinces with counties in Xinjiang, matching workers to companies and offering subsidies to participating businesses. UN experts warned in January that the programmes can amount to forced labour and are being used to "forcibly re-engineer" Uyghur identity. Many workers are monitored and Mandarin lessons and ideological training are standard parts of the programmes.
China's foreign ministry said the purpose of the programme is to raise income levels and eradicate poverty, and said "Xinjiang has never forced anyone to transfer employment." Forced labour, it added, is strictly prohibited.
Yet researchers and rights groups say the transfers form part of a broader effort to reshape Uyghur society.
The objective is to "disconnect Uyghurs from their heritage, their traditional way of life, and their families, and to politically indoctrinate them to Han Chinese culture", says Uluyol of Human Rights Watch. Experts say that the labour-transfer programme, together with birth prevention policies and encouraging Han Chinese to move to the region, is part of state efforts to "optimise" the Uyghur population.
O ver the past decade, southern Xinjiang has been transformed. The loss of working-age adults to detention and labour transfers is hollowing out villages across the region, says Zenz. Those left behind are often elderly, sick or very young.
With information from Xinjiang so tightly controlled, new details are still emerging about the mechanisms authorities use to suppress Uyghur culture. There used to be 10 different Uyghur-language publishers, but they have gone, says Steenberg. The books have been "removed from bookstores ... they've been removed from people's homes, they've been removed from libraries in huge stacks."
Even the books at Xinjiang University, famous for its Uyghur language collection, were destroyed, fed into a wood chipper in the space of a month in early 2018, says Ayup.
The remaining books in Uyghur are often Chinese works translated into the language, including state propaganda. Similar methods of erasure are used in other cultural domains. Steenberg adds: "We still have Uvghur-language TV series, but if you look at the content, it's basically Chinese sitcoms translated into Uyghur."
Mosques remain tightly controlled, according to the former university worker, who described how authorities would order locals to attend prayers for photographs and videos. Mosque managers, including one they knew, would go door to door collecting signatures to fabricate attendance records for officials to show there was no religious persecution.
China denies "eliminating" Uyghur language, culture and religious practices, and says they are both protected and promoted throughout Xinjiang. But messaging from Beijing emphasises a push for "ethnic unity". A new law downgrades languages other than Mandarin, encourages inter-ethnic marriage and requires parents to raise children to "love the Chinese Communist Party".
"Everything that once reflected Uyghur civilisation - our culture, our language - has been wiped out," says a Uyghur man who recently visited the region from the US.
