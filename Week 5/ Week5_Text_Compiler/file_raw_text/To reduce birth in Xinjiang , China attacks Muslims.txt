When the government ordered that women in its mostly Muslim community would have to receive contraceptive devices, Qelbinur Sedik asked to be exempt from the measure. He told authorities in Xinjiang that he was almost 50 years old. He had obeyed the birth limits set by the government and had only one child.
It didn't do any good. The staff threatened to take her to the police if she continued to resist. Sedik said he surrendered and went to a government clinic where a doctor, using metal forceps, inserted in her an IUD (intrauterine device) contraceptive. She cried as she went through the procedure.

"I felt like I stopped being a normal woman," Sedik said, his voice in a statement, describing the suffering she went through in 2017. "As if something was missing in me."
In much of China, authorities are encouraging women to have more children, seeking to avoid a predicted demographic crisis due to the fall in the birth rate. But in Xinjiang, the country's far western region, they are forcing women to have less in an effort to tighten the siege around Muslim ethnic minorities.
It is part of a vast and repressive campaign of social reengineering waged by a Communist Party determined to eliminate anything it sees as a challenge to its dominance - in the case of Xinjiang, ethnic separatism.

In recent years, the party, under the command of top leader Xi Jinping, has taken aggressive steps to control Uighurs and other Central Asian minorities in Xinjiang, sending hundreds of thousands of them to detention camps and prisons. The authorities imposed a strict surveillance regime in the region, sent the inhabitants to work in factories and children, to boarding school.
When they turn to repression against Muslim women, the authorities are going even further, seeking to orchestrate a demographic transformation that will affect the population for generations. The birth rate has already fallen sharply in the region in recent years with the use of invasive birth control procedures, facts previously documented by a researcher, Adrian Zenz, working with the Associated Press agency.
Authorities have said the procedures are voluntary, but interviews with more than a dozen Uighurs, Kazakhs and other Xinjiang Muslims, women and men, as well as an analysis of official statistics, government-released warnings and reports published in state media, reveal a coercive effort by the Chinese Communist Party to control the community's reproductive rights.
Authorities pressured women to use IUDs or be sterilized. When they recovered at home, government officials were sent to live with them to observe possible signs of dissatisfaction. One woman described how she had to endure being groped by the government official.
If they had had too many children or refused contraceptive procedures, they had to pay heavy fines or, even worse, be detained in internment camps. In the camps, women were at risk of further abuse. Some former detainees in these camps said they were forced to take medications that interrupted their menstrual cycles. A woman said she was raped in an internment camp.
For human rights advocates and Western officials, the Chinese government-led crackdown in Xinjiang amounts to crimes against humanity and genocide, largely due to efforts to curb the population growth of Muslim minorities. The Trump administration in January was the first government to declare the crackdown a genocide, with reproductive oppression being the main reason. The Biden administration reaffirmed its qualifications in March.
Reported in The Guardian and other publications, Sedik's testimony helped substantiate the U.S. government's decision. "It was one of the most detailed and compelling first-person testimonials we had heard," said Kelley E. Currie, a former U.S. ambassador who participated in the government's discussions. "Her account helped give a human face to the appalling statistics we were seeing."
Beijing accuses its critics of promoting an anti-China agenda.
The Chinese government says the recent decline in the region's birth rate stems from the full implementation of long-standing birth restrictions. Sterilizations and contraceptive procedures, he said, free women from retrograde attitudes toward reproduction and religion.
"The use of contraceptive methods and the method chosen are determined entirely by the free will of women," a government spokesman, Xu Guixiang, said at a press conference in March. "No one and no organ will interfere."
"I've lost all hope"
Qelbinur Sedik, of Uzbek ethnicity, has always seen himself as a model citizen.
After graduating from college, she married and devoted herself to the work, teaching the Chinese language to Uighur primary school students. He edifily read the rules, he waited for his employer's permission to get pregnant. She only had a daughter in 1993.
Sedik could have had two children. The rules of the time allowed members of ethnic minorities to have slightly larger families than those of the majority Chinese ethnic group, the Hans, especially in the countryside. The government even gave Sedik a certificate of honor for pushing the boundaries.
But everything changed in 2017.
At the same time that the government cornered Uighurs and Kazakhs in mass internment camps, it hardened the implementation of birth controls. Between 2015 and 2018 the sterilization rate in Xinjiang multiplied by almost six, reaching just over 60,000 procedures. At the same time, according to Zenz's calculations, sterilizations have declined radically in the rest of the country.
The xinjiang campaign is departing from a broader government-led effort since 2015 to encourage birth, including offering tax incentives and removing IUDs free of charge. But between 2015 and 2018, new IUD insertions increased in Xinjiang, while devices were becoming less and less used across the country.

The contraception campaign seems to have worked.
According to Zenz's calculations, the birth rate in counties in the region populated mainly by ethnic minorities fell sharply between 2015 and 2018. Several of those counties stopped disclosing demographic data, but Zenz calculated that in 2019 the birth rate in minority areas continued to decline, falling just over 50% compared to 2018.
Judging by beijing, the campaign is a victory for Muslim women in the region.
"The deradicalization process has also led to the release of some female minds," says a January report from a research center in Xinjiang. "Women have avoided the suffering of being imprisoned by extremism and converted into reproductive tools."
Women like Sedik, who had obeyed the rules, were not spared. After the IUD insertion, Sedik suffered severe bleeding and headaches. He later removed the device in secret, but then reinserted it. In 2019 she decided to sterilize herself.
"The government had become so rigid, and I couldn't stand the IUD anymore," said Sedik, who fled China in 2019 and now lives in the Netherlands. "I've lost all hope."
"The women of Xinjiang are in danger"
The penalties for those who disobey the government are heavy. A Han Chinese woman who broke birth regulations would be fined, but a Uighur or Kazakh woman who did so would risk being detained.
When Gulnar Omirzakh had his third child in 2015, his village authorities recorded the birth. Three years later, however, she was told that she had violated the child limit and owed $2,700 in fines.
If Omirzakh did not pay the fines, they said, she and her two daughters would be detained. She borrowed money from her relatives. He later fled to Kazakhstan.
"The women of Xinjiang are in danger," Omirazkh said in a telephone interview. "The government wants to replace our people."
The threat of arrest was real.
Three women told the NYT that they met other inmate squats in internment camps who had been detained for violating birth restrictions.
The ex-inmates' accounts could not be verified by an independent source because strict restrictions in Xinjiang make it impossible to free access to internment camps. The Chinese government strongly rejects all allegations of abuses in the camps.
"Sexual attacks and torture cannot exist," xu, the regional spokesman, said at a press conference in February.
While claiming to defend women's rights, Beijing seeks to question the credibility of women who have come out to make accusations, accusing them of lying and immorality.

Not even at home did women feel safe. Representatives of the Chinese Communist Party attended their homes uninvited, and they were forced to let them in.
As part of a campaign titled "Form doubles and come families," the party sent more than 1 million employees to make regular visits to Muslim homes, sometimes to stay with them. For many Uighurs, these representatives are nothing but spies.
Representatives had to inform authorities if the families they visited showed signs of "extremist behavior." In the case of women, this included any resentment they might have felt for being subject to state-imposed contraceptive procedures.
When party representatives came to his home in 2018, Zumret Dawut had just been fore-sterilized by force.
Four Hans's representatives visited her in Urumqi, she recalled, taking yogurt and eggs to help her recover. And they also came armed with questions: did she have any complaints to make about the sterilization operation? Were you dissatisfied with government policy?
"I was too afraid to send myself back to the camps if I said something wrong," said Dawut, a mother of three. "Then I simply said, 'We are all Chinese and we have to do what Chinese law says.'"
