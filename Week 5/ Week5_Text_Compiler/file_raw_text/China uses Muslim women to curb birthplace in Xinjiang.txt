When China's government ordered contraceptive devices to be placed on women in the Muslim community in the Xinjiang region, Qelbinur Sedik pleaded with them to dispense it. He was almost 50 years old, he told the officials. He had respected the government-imposed child limit and had only one daughter.
It was useless. The workers threatened to take her to the police if she kept resisting, she said. He then agreed to go to a government clinic where with a metal forceps a doctor placed an intrauterine device (IUD) on him to prevent him from getting pregnant. She stood still crying throughout the process.
"I felt like I was no longer a normal woman," Sedik remarked with a lump in her throat as she recounted the terrible experience of 2017. "Like they're taking something from me."
In a large part of China, the authorities are encouraging women to have more children to try to avoid a demographic crisis as a result of a declining birth rate. But in the Xinjiang region, China is forcing them to have fewer children, reinforcing their control over Muslim ethnic minorities and attempting to undertake a demographic transformation that will diminish their population over the generations.
According to reports from a renowned researcher, Adrian Zenz, along with those of The Associated Press, as the use of invasive birth control procedures has increased, birth rates in that region have fallen in recent years.
This is part of a vast and repressive Communist Party campaign for social redesign bent on removing any alleged obstacles to its government; in this case, ethnic separatism. Over the past few years, the party, led by its leader, Xi Jinping, has been tasked with putting Uighurs and other Central Asian minorities very strongly in Xinjiang by interning hundreds of thousands of them in prison camps and prisons. The authorities have placed this region under strict surveillance, sent their residents to work in factories and moved children to boarding school.
Although the authorities have said birth control procedures are voluntary, interviews with more than a dozen Uighurs, Kazakhs and other Muslims in Xinjiang, as well as an analysis of official statistics, government announcements and reports in the state media reveal attempts at coerciveness by the Communist Party of China to impede the reproductive rights of the community. Authorities forced women to use an IUD or sterilize therself. Government officials sent their home to live with them as they recovered for the purpose of detecting signs of nonconformity. One woman mentioned having to endure the touching of her watchman.
If they had too many children or rejected contraception proceedings, they would be credited with heavy fines or, even worse, to be confined to a detention camp. In these fields, women were at risk of even more abuse. Some women who were previously arrested argue that they were made to take medications to interrupt their menstrual cycle. A woman claimed to have been raped in one of these camps.
For human rights defenders and Western authorities, largely due to attempts to stop the population growth of Muslim minorities, government repression in Xinjiang amounts to crimes against humanity and genocide. In January, the Trump administration was the first to classify these severe measures as genocide; one of the main reasons was the imposition on reproductive issues. In March, Biden's government confirmed this designation.
Sedik's experience, narrated in The Guardian and other media, served as the basis for the US government's decision. "It was one of the most detailed and compelling first-person stories we saw," said Kelley E. Currie, who served as U.S. ambassador and participated in government debates. "He helped put a face on the chilling statistics we were seeing."
Beijing has accused its opponents of pushing an agenda against China.
The government has said that recent declines in birth rates in that region were the result of the authorities rigorously applying the old birth restrictions. He argued that contraceptive and sterilization procedures freed women from retrograde customs about procreation and religion.
"They completely decide whether to have birth control or what method of contraception to use," Xu Guixiang, xinjiang government spokesman, said at a March press conference. "Neither any organism or person should intervene."
'I lost all hope in myself'
Sedik, belonging to the Uzbek ethnic group, had all her life considered he an exemplary citizen.
When he graduated from college, he married and set to work teaching Chinese to primary-level Uighur children. Obedient to the rules, Sedik did not get pregnant until his employer gave him permission. He only had one daughter, in 1993.
Sedik could have had two children. At the time, the rules allowed ethnic minority families to be slightly larger than those of the Chinese ethnic group, which was the majority, especially in rural areas. The government even gave Sedik a certificate of honor for staying within limits.
Then everything changed in 2017.
When the government interned Uighurs and Kazakhs in mass detention camps, the implementation of birth control measures increased in parallel. According to Zenz's calculations, from 2015 to 2018, sterilization rates in Xinjiang increased nearly six times, to just over 60,000 procedures, although they decreased greatly across the country.
Xinjiang's campaign is in conflict with a more widespread government effort since 2015 to promote increased birth rate, which includes granting tax subsidies and withdrawing IUDs at no cost. But from 2015 to 2018, Xinjiang increased the number of new IUDs placed across the country, even though the use of these devices declined sharply nationally.
It looked like the birth control campaign was working.
According to Zenz's estimates, birth rates fell from 2015 to 2018 in county counties where minorities predominated. Many of these counties have stopped publishing birth data, but based on figures from other countries, Zenz estimated that birth rates in minority areas continued to decline in 2019 by more than 50 percent compared to 2018.
According to Beijing, the campaign is a triumph for Muslim women in that region.
"The process against radicalization has also freed up the mindset of some women," said a January report from a research center in the Xinjiang government. "They have saved the suffering of being prey to extremism and being turned into breeding tools."
They did not forgive women who had obeyed the rules, like Sedik. After the IUD was placed, Sedik had severe bleeding and headaches. He then had his device removed clandestinely and then replaced. In 2019, she decided to have it sterilized.
"The government had become very strict and I could no longer continue with the IUD," said Sedik, who now lives in the Netherlands after fleeing China in 2019. "I lost all hope in myself."
'Women in Xinjiang are in danger'
Sanctions for not obeying the government were excessive. A woman of the ethnic group had violated birth rules, was fined, while perhaps a Uighur or Kazakh woman could be arrested.
When Gulnar Omirzakh had his third child in 2015, authorities in his northern village recorded the birth. But three years later, they said he hadn't respected the birth limit and owed $2700 in fines.
Authorities said if she didn't pay, they'd stop her and her two daughters.
He borrowed money from his relatives and then fled to Kazakhstan.
"The women of Xinjiang are in danger," Omirzakh said in a telephone interview. "The government wants to replace our people."
The threat of arrest was real.
Three women told The New York Times that in prison camps they had met other women who had been locked up for not respecting birth restrictions.
Due to strict restrictions in Xinjiang, free access to the camps was not possible and we were unable to corroborate on our own the accounts of previously arrested women. The Chinese government has defied allallegations of abuse at their facilities.
"There can be no sexual violence or torture," Xu, the region's spokesman, said at a February press conference.
Beijing has wanted to weaken the credibility of women who have raised their voices by ensuring that they lie and have bad principles while claiming to defend women's rights.
'We're all Chinese'
Women didn't feel safe even in their homes. Suddenly brigades of the Communist Party of China were presented and had to let them in.
As part of a campaign called "Join and Join the Family," the party regularly sends more than a million workers to visit and sometimes stay in The Muslims' homes. For many Uighurs, there wasn't much difference between brigades and spies.
These brigades were tasked with reporting whether the families they visited showed signs of "extremist behaviors." In the case of women, this included any resentment they may have over state-ordered contraceptive procedures.
When the party brigades arrived in 2018, Zumret Dawut had just been forerated by force.
She remembers that in Urumchi, she was visited by four brigades who brought yogurt and eggs to help her recovery. They also arrived equipped with questions: Did you have a problem with the sterilization procedure? Was he unhappy with government policy?
"I was very afraid that if I said something inappropriate they would send me back to the camps," said Dawut, a mother of three. "So I just told them , 'We're all Chinese and we have to do what The Laws of China say.'"
