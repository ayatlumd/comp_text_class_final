Body


Chained students. Women raped in herd by security guards who sweat xenophobia. Torture, torment. Deaths by suffocation. These are some of the sufferings to which members of Muslim minorities are subjected in Xinjiang, China, and that a CNN news team - led by journalists Ivan Watson and Rebecca Wright - was able to collect thanks to the bravery of three protagonists and witnesses to those old men. All of them, women.
In that region controlled by the Beijing regime, the most abominable human rights violations that very few countries have been encouraged to point out, for fear of economic retaliation by the Asian giant, occur. The Uighur minority is the victim of genocide, according to Washington, while China indicates that these are only re-education centers and denies any accusations against them: "an anti-Chinese campaign," they argue.
"There is no systematic sexual assault or abuse against women in Xinjiang," the Chinese government said in response to the US news network. However, the courageous testimonies of women who managed to get out of these centres alive and others who participated in the teaching that was being taught there and who are no longer there, confirm the most horrendous crimes committed against them.
One of them decided to speak so that the world would know the aberrations that were committed to subdue the Uighurs in that remote region of the country. Qelbinur Sidik taught children at a school there. One day, without much explanation, she was forging the force to teach Mandarin to the detainees for three months. You'll never forget that traumatic experience. Learning the language is one of the conditions that the regional authorities impose on those who settle there.
He began his obligatory work in March 2017. She went from caring for children between the age of six and thirteen to older adults. When he first saw them, he couldn't believe it: they were all chained, feet and hands, as if they were high-danger criminals. Sidik, a Uzbek who had lived there for almost 30 years, did not quite understand why if those she had in front of her were like her, migrants in search of a better life they did not find.
The teacher recounted some of the horror. "When the guards drank at night, the cops told each other how they raped and tortured the girls," she said. Sidik saw with his own eyes how a woman died as a result of these group rapes. In a herd. "There was no spark of life on his face. His cheeks were colorless, he wasn't breathing," he recalled. The victim was carried by other guards and a policewoman confirmed to her what had happened: torture, rape and the consequent murder.
The transformation of the detainees was evident over the days. Those "students in the process of re-education" who first arrived at the center where she worked looked healthy, in good physical condition and well fed. However, within a few days, it was all skin and bones. On many occasions, when he turned around to write on the board, he listened to many of those present cry in shame. He also heard other sounds, more guttural and strange, coming from other rooms in the building. There were no classes there, just torture.
After the traumatic experience, Sidik decided to emigrate from China. He now lives with his family in Holland.
Tursunay Ziyawudun was (is) one of the victims. She was arrested in April 2017 for the first time. She had committed no crime, only wearing her veil and being seen as a woman who fulfilled religious mandates. She was taken to Xinyuan County Vocational School in Xinjiang. "Vocational" is the euphemism that the regime uses to soften the trueidentity of their detention camps. She was in jail for a month. But a year later, she was again taken hostage.
Ziyawudun was in a cell with 20 other women. During the day I received some food and water. To the bathroom, he recounted, he could go for up to five minutes a day. If it went too far in time inside the toilet, it could suffer consequences: "Those who spent the most time received electric shocks with canes," he said.
The interrogations, strange to be an alleged vocational school, were endless. The guards wanted to know why she remained with her husband Halmirza Halik in Kazakhstan for five years before returning to China in 2017. They were doing it to the punches. But in one of those sessions hell was complete: Ziyawudun claimed that two female guards took her to another room where she was placed on a table. "They inserted a paralyzing cane inside me and twisted me and electrocuted me with it. I fainted."
Ten days later, the worst came back. She was taken out of her cell and taken somewhere else. "In the next room I heard another girl crying and screaming. I saw about five or six men entering that room. I thought she was being tortured. But then I was raped as a group. After that I realized what they had done to her, too. They were extremely sadistic, causing pain and damage to the body by hitting me and hitting my head against the wall... was his way of punishing us," said the victim who now lives in America.
He arrived in America after a long journey. She was released and September 2019 was warned by the regime: she could not talk to anyone about what she had experienced in that "vocational" center. She then began the way of the reunion with her husband, who was waiting for her on the border with Kazakhstan. But there, in the following days and as a result of the multiple rapes, he had severe vaginal bleeding. Once on the other side of the border, she was taken by plane to the United States, where she managed to survive.
Another Uighur interviewed by CNN is Gulbakhar Jalilova. He also lived the same ordeal. She decided to face one of her jailers. "I said, 'Aren't you ashamed? Don't you have a mother, a sister? How can you do this to me like that?' He hit me with the electroshock itch and said, 'You don't look like a human.'"
